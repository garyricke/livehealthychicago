Everyone at the Table.

On October 26, the whole Live Healthy Chicago coalition came together in one room for the first time: clinicians, community health workers, public health leaders and neighborhood partners. We asked them why this work matters, and why it works.

Relationships come before medicine

The mobile team learned in year one that trust has to come first. Year two leans into listening.
  • Listening sessions and Community Advisory Boards give structured feedback that shapes the clinical approach.
  • Partners are working through leaders at community organizations and the community health workers already on site.
  • More staff time and money is going into community building before anyone talks medication.
“It’s a complex problem to try to get people that have issues with food insecurity and violence in the home to care about an asymptomatic risk factor like hypertension.”Daniel Luger

Celebrate the small wins

Someone keeping an appointment, or just listening for 15 minutes, counts as progress.
  • Much of this work happens in men’s and women’s shelters, with people who move around a lot.
  • Each conversation builds on the last until the relationship can reach the root causes.
“Just keep dropping them grains of sand. Next thing you know, you got a beautiful beach.”Cornelius Charles

Beyond the resource line

People reliably come out for back-to-school drives and resource fairs. Getting them back for screenings and health workshops is the real challenge.
  • Many people don’t have the time to come out for things that aren’t an immediate need.
  • Health is tied to everything else: fresh food, nearby doctors, and community “third spaces.”
  • History explains why some communities don’t trust the health care system, so speaking the community’s language matters.
“How do we build that trust, but then build those bridges to make this a convenient experience for them as well?”Michael Anjum-Turner
Heard from

A safe space to be yourself

The goal is a space where people can be fully themselves, not a checklist of techniques.
  • Active listening and open-ended questions only go so far. What moves the needle is people feeling cared for.
  • Partners also want community members to say what they want to see in their own neighborhoods, and then help them make it happen.
Heard from

Fear of the unknown

Many people avoid finding out their numbers because doctors and medication feel unfamiliar.
  • Some people have never been on medication or had an annual physical.
  • Patients who get over that first hurdle often come back weekly, more than the program asks, to share how they’re doing.
“Some people really do push it off and they don’t want to know.”Trisha Patel
Heard from

Meet people where they are

The phrase we heard most. Care goes to workplaces, residences, food pantries and libraries.
  • The mobile team serves people who aren’t connected to any provider and may be avoiding hospitals and clinics.
  • CDPH community health workers are at Chicago Public Libraries and food pantries week after week.
  • Rush’s community teams bring care to where people live, work and play.
“Instead of expecting people to come to Rush, we go to where they are.”Dr. Elizabeth Davis
Heard from

Know your number

Many people don’t know their blood pressure, or what counts as high. Education happens even when someone doesn’t enroll.
  • Every visit covers the goal reading and the diet and lifestyle changes that help.
  • A dangerously high reading means a same-day push to emergency care.
“Just get your blood pressure checked. … It’s better to know, and once you know, you can act on it.”Trisha Patel
Heard from

How the mobile care team works

Screening first, then a pharmacist on site, then three months of support before a hand-off to primary care.
  • Medical students and residents build connections and screen at community organizations.
  • Once enough people are engaged, a pharmacist comes on site to prescribe, order labs, set up blood pressure monitoring and adjust medication.
  • Patients check in every two weeks for three months, then move to a primary care doctor.
Heard from

Practical help, in the moment

Small, concrete fixes keep people on track.
  • Charities help pay for medication people can’t afford.
  • When someone loses their medicine, CHWs help them call the doctor and pharmacy for a refill.
  • Libraries curate books on cutting salt and starting exercise, and people are connected to the Illinois Tobacco Quitline for free nicotine replacement.
  • One man swapped pop for two to three Gatorades a day, not knowing about the sodium. He cut back, and two weeks later his numbers were down.
“It’s not what we think it is. It’s how they need the support.”Kate Paz
Heard from

Community health workers who share the experience

Residents connect with CHWs who look like them and have lived what they have.
  • CHWs connect people with high readings to the mobile clinics.
  • Support follows the person’s needs, not a script.
Heard from

Support between visits

The Nuna app helps patients and care teams connect what happens at home with what happens at the visit.
  • Care teams can adjust a care plan based on what patients log at home.
  • Knowing the care team can see their progress gives patients an extra nudge.
“Oftentimes patients don’t know where to go or where to start. And Nuna helps bridge that gap for them.”Jay Melnyk
Heard from

A team-based standard of care

Hypertension care is moving from the single doctor to the whole care team, with one standard across the city.
  • Physicians trained one way are rethinking how they treat hypertension.
  • The aim is one standard of care in South and West Side health centers, no matter where you get care.
“We’ve been doing it wrong for many years.”Dr. Octavio Vega

The coalition chose to stay

When the original funding ended, partners stayed at the table.
  • Dr. David Ansell told the room how the coalition nearly fell apart, and didn’t.
  • Institutions that usually compete are sharing what works.
“When we sit at a table together, we are looking to innovate, regardless of the institution that we’re from.”Allie Sontag

Built to flex for the next crisis

Public health money is usually tied to one disease. Live Healthy Chicago is designed to move with whatever the city needs next.
  • Hypertension is today’s focus, but it doesn’t have to be the only one.
  • Only a handful of citywide public-private coalitions like this exist.
“If we’re all sitting at the table together, we can speed up our city’s ability to respond.”Allie Sontag
Heard from

Big tents for big problems

The coalition brings together FQHCs, health systems, community organizations, the city and technology partners.
  • CDPH has a new Deputy Commissioner of Population Health and Health Equity to lead hyper-local work.
  • The CARE program pairs a first responder with a mental health clinician, and CDPH plans to add community health workers.
  • IMAN shows the value of a one-stop hub: housing, a grocery store, a food pantry, a health center and behavioral health.
“For big problems, we need to make sure that we have big tents.”Dr. Garth Walker
Heard from

Growing the workforce

Training pipelines are expanding.
  • UChicago Medicine is setting up CHW credentialing as the state allows more CHW billing.
  • Rush’s mobile team draws on dozens of medical students eager to do community work.
Heard from

80% blood pressure control

Dr. Vega’s hope: hypertension control at 80% or higher across the South and West sides, and the city.
  • Better control means lower cardiovascular risk for each patient.

  • The work has moved from planning to practice, and some participants’ blood pressure has already improved.
