Understanding High Blood Pressure

Blood pressure is the force your blood uses to move through your vessels. You need it. The goal was never low blood pressure — it’s healthy blood pressure.

Two ways to explain it

Tap a card

What uncontrolled high blood pressure damages

Raises the risk of heart attack and heart failure.
Raises the risk of stroke, and problems with memory and thinking.
Makes it harder for them to filter waste out of the blood.
Can lead to vision problems, and even vision loss.
Makes it harder for blood to reach the organs that need it.

Risk factors

Can’t be changed
  • Getting older
  • Family history
  • Certain conditions — diabetes, chronic kidney disease
  • Population risk — African American adults often develop it younger, with more severe complications
Can be changed
  • Eating a heart-healthy diet
  • Reducing sodium
  • Being physically active
  • Managing stress
  • Getting enough sleep
  • Avoiding tobacco
  • Limiting alcohol
  • Taking medication as prescribed
  • Keeping healthcare appointments

Having a risk factor doesn’t mean someone will develop high blood pressure. It means knowing their numbers matters even more.

This isn’t caused by one single thing. Genetics, chronic stress, economic conditions, access to healthcare and other social factors all shape someone’s blood pressure.

The one message to remember

High blood pressure is common, and once people know their numbers, understand their risk, and feel supported, there’s no reason they can’t protect their body.

Preventing and Managing

Nobody has to change everything at once. Small, consistent changes become lasting habits far more reliably than trying to overhaul a life overnight.

Where the sodium is actually hiding

Most of it never comes from the salt shaker.

Check labels. Rinsing canned beans or vegetables cuts sodium further.
Often a full day’s sodium in a single tray.
A breakfast sandwich alone can be half the daily limit.
A deli sandwich and chips can push someone past the whole day’s recommendation.

Honouring the food, changing the method

Tap a card

Healthy eating isn’t about changing someone’s culture. It’s about cooking the food they love in a way that loves them back.

Start by listening

  • Instead ofWhy aren’t you eating healthier? → TryWhat’s making healthy eating difficult for you?

That one question changes the conversation. Once you understand the barrier, you can help find a realistic solution — frozen vegetables, low-sodium canned goods, a rotisserie chicken and microwave rice on a long shift.

Movement, stress, sleep and medication

Walking, stairs, gardening, dancing, playing with grandchildren, chair exercises. Three 10-minute walks count. Every minute counts.
Don’t ask them to change their schedule. Ask where five or ten minutes could fit — marching during a call, dancing while cooking dinner.
Constant stress releases hormones that raise blood pressure, and makes every other habit harder.
Aim for 7–9 hours. Poor sleep and sleep apnea make blood pressure harder to control.
Blood pressure medicine controls high blood pressure — it doesn’t cure it. If cost or side effects are the problem, get them to their provider or pharmacist before they stop.
The one message to remember

Healthy habits don’t have to be perfect. They have to be realistic, consistent and sustainable. Help people pick one change they can start today.

Know Your Numbers

Clap your hands. When they come together, that’s the heart squeezing — the top number. When they separate, that’s the heart relaxing — the bottom number.

The American Heart Association categories

Blood pressure categories, systolic over diastolic, in mmHg
CategorySystolic and/orDiastolic
Normal Less than 120andLess than 80
Elevated 120–129andLess than 80
Stage 1 hypertension 130–139or80–89
Stage 2 hypertension 140 or higheror90 or higher
Hypertensive crisis Higher than 180and/orHigher than 120

Your role isn’t to diagnose. It’s to recognise when someone should follow up — and when a reading needs attention right now.

Taking an accurate reading

Before
  • Rest quietly for 5 minutes
  • No smoking for 30 minutes
  • No caffeine for 30 minutes
  • No exercise for 30 minutes
  • Empty your bladder
During
  • Back supported
  • Feet flat on the floor
  • Legs uncrossed
  • Arm resting at heart level
  • Cuff on a bare arm, not over clothing
  • Correct cuff size
  • Don’t talk
After
  • Record it — notebook, phone app, or the monitor’s memory
  • Bring the readings to appointments
  • One reading is a snapshot; several tell the real story

Don’t worry about memorising every number. Aim for normal. If it stays high over time, help them get to a provider. If it’s extremely high, act — that’s Module 4.

The one message to remember

Knowing your numbers matters, but only if they’re accurate. The steps before, during and after a reading are what make it reliable enough to act on.

Recognising an Emergency

Not every high reading is an emergency. But a reading over 180/120 needs attention, and what happens next depends entirely on whether the person has symptoms.

180/120 Higher than this? Start here.
  1. 1
    Stay calm. Wait one minute. Check again.

    A second reading is often lower once someone has rested.

  2. 2
    Still above 180/120? Look for symptoms.
    • Chest pain
    • Shortness of breath
    • Back pain
    • Numbness
    • Weakness
    • Vision changes
    • Difficulty speaking
Symptoms present

Call 911 immediately. Don’t wait to see if it comes down on its own. This may be a hypertensive emergency, and every minute matters.

No symptoms

This is severe hypertension. It usually doesn’t need 911, but it does need prompt medical attention — help them contact their provider as soon as possible.

BE FAST

The stroke warning signs. Tap a letter.

The most important letter is T. Time. Don’t drive someone to the hospital yourself unless it’s the only option — call 911 so treatment can start on arrival.

The one message to remember

High blood pressure doesn’t always mean an emergency — but it should never be ignored. Knowing what to do in those first few minutes can save a life.

Turning Knowledge into Action

Community health workers do much more than share information. You build relationships. You earn trust. You help people feel heard — and people change when they feel respected and understood.

Change the question, change the answer

  • Instead ofWhy aren’t you taking your medication? → TryWhat’s making it difficult to take your medication?
  • Instead ofWhy aren’t you eating healthier? → TryWhat makes healthy eating difficult for you?
  • Instead ofDo you understand? → TryCan you tell me, in your own words, what you’ll do when you get home?
  • Instead ofWhy didn’t you do it? → TryWhat got in the way?

Introducing the word difficult makes a question land as help rather than judgement.

The teach-back method

Teach-back checks whether you explained it clearly — not whether they were paying attention.
“Just so I know I explained it clearly, can you tell me in your own words what you’ll do when you get home?”
Try a different way. That’s the whole point of asking.

The CHW toolbox

  • Listen first.
  • Teach in simple language.
  • Use teach-back.
  • Help people choose one realistic goal.
  • Encourage progress, not perfection.
  • Know when to refer to a provider.

When to refer to a healthcare provider

Encourage them to contact their provider.
Provider or pharmacist — before they stop taking anything.
There may be other options that work better for them.
Anything new is worth a call.
Call 911 immediately. Don’t wait.
The one message to remember

Knowledge matters, but how we share it matters just as much. Never underestimate the power of a conversation. One blood pressure check. One healthier meal. One person who finally feels heard.

One teaspoon. A whole day.

Milligrams don’t mean much to most people. So make it something they can hold.

1teaspoon of table salt =  2,300mg of sodium

That is the maximum most adults should have in an entire day. Not one meal — the whole day. Most people are surprised.

A fast-food breakfast sandwich alone can be about half of it. Add a deli sandwich and chips at lunch, and many people are already over the limit before the afternoon starts.

And most of it never came from the salt shaker — it came from packaged food, canned food, frozen meals and restaurant meals. Once people know where the sodium is hiding, they can start making different choices.
